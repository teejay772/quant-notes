Sharpe ratio estimation

The Sharpe ratio is the mean of a strategy's excess returns divided by their standard deviation. This page states the definition and its annualisation, the sampling error of the estimate, the inflation of the best estimate among several trials, and two published corrections: the deflated Sharpe ratio and the multiple-testing haircut. Each section is followed by a lab on simulated returns.

Constraints 1 · Signals use only past data. 2 · The one-bar lag, pos = target.shift(1), is never removed. 3 · Every result includes costs. A Sharpe ratio reported by the project is computed on returns produced under all three.

All series on this page are simulated, not market data. No performance is reported. The worked examples in sections 4 and 5 are the hypothetical examples published in the cited papers.

1 · Definition

Let r1, …, rT be a strategy's returns over T periods and rf the risk-free return over one period. With μ̂ the sample mean and σ̂ the sample standard deviation of the excess returns rt − rf, the Sharpe ratio per period is

SR̂ = μ̂ − rfσ̂

Read aloud: the estimated Sharpe ratio is the average return in excess of the risk-free rate, divided by the standard deviation of those returns. The hat on a symbol marks a quantity estimated from a sample.

Sharpe ratios are quoted in annual units so that strategies sampled at different frequencies can be compared. With q periods per year and returns independent and identically distributed (IID), Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado and Zhu (2014, eq. 2) give

SRannual = μσ · √q

Read aloud: the annual Sharpe ratio is the per-period ratio of mean to standard deviation, multiplied by the square root of the number of periods in a year. Daily data with one row per calendar day has q = 365.

Harvey and Liu (2015, eq. 1–2) connect the ratio to the t-statistic for the null hypothesis that the mean return is zero:

t = μ̂σ̂ / √T = SR̂ · √T

Read aloud: the t-statistic is the per-period Sharpe ratio times the square root of the number of observations. For a fixed sample length, a higher Sharpe ratio means a higher t-statistic and a smaller p-value.

2 · Annualisation

Over q periods the mean of a sum of returns is q times the per-period mean. If the returns are uncorrelated, the variance of the sum is q times the per-period variance, so the standard deviation grows with √q. The ratio of the two therefore grows with q / √q = √q. Lo and MacKinlay (1988, p. 44) state the underlying property: "the variance of the increments of a random walk is linear in the observation interval."

When returns are autocorrelated the variance of the q-period sum is no longer q times the one-period variance. Lo and MacKinlay measure the departure with the variance ratio VR(q), the variance of q-period returns divided by q times the variance of one-period returns. Their equation 16 expresses it through the autocorrelations ρ(k):

VR(q) ≈ 1 + Σk = 1 … q−1 2 (q − k)q ρ(k)

Read aloud: the variance ratio is one plus a weighted sum of the autocorrelations at lags 1 to q − 1, with weights that decline in a straight line from nearly 2 at lag 1 to 2/q at lag q − 1. Positive autocorrelation makes VR(q) greater than 1; negative autocorrelation makes it less than 1.

SRannual = μ · qσ · √q · VR(q) = (μ/σ) · √q√VR(q)

Read aloud: with autocorrelation, the annual Sharpe ratio equals the √q-scaled ratio divided by the square root of the variance ratio. The √q rule overstates the annual Sharpe ratio when returns are positively autocorrelated and understates it when they are negatively autocorrelated.

3 · Sampling error

The true Sharpe ratio is a property of the process that generates returns and is not observed. A backtest of y years returns an estimate, and a different y-year history from the same process would return a different estimate. For IID Normal returns, Bailey et al. (2014, eq. 3), citing Lo (2002), give the asymptotic distribution of the annualised estimate:

SR̂ → N ( SR , 1 + SR22qy )

Read aloud: as the sample grows, the estimated annual Sharpe ratio is approximately Normal, centred on the true value, with variance one plus SR squared over 2q, all divided by the number of years y. The standard error falls with the square root of the number of years, not with the number of observations: sampling daily instead of monthly does not shorten the history required.

A 95% interval is approximately SR̂ ± 1.96 standard errors. The interval excludes zero only when y exceeds 1.96² (1 + SR²/2q) / SR². For a true annual Sharpe ratio of 0.5 that is – years; for 1.0 it is – years. The formula assumes IID Normal returns. For returns with autocorrelation and changing volatility, the stationary bootstrap of Politis and Romano (1994) resamples blocks of consecutive returns of random, geometrically distributed length, and is the method the research plan applies to the project's own returns.

4 · Selection among trials

A trial is one backtested configuration: a rule with one set of parameters. If N independent trials are run and the configuration with the highest Sharpe ratio is retained, the retained estimate is the maximum of N noisy estimates, and it exceeds zero even when every true Sharpe ratio is zero. For N independent standard Normal variables, Bailey et al. (2014, eq. 4) approximate the expected maximum as

E[maxN] ≈ (1 − γ) Z−1[1 − 1N] + γ Z−1[1 − 1Ne−1]

Read aloud: the expected largest of N standard Normal draws is a weighted combination of two Normal quantiles, with weights set by the Euler–Mascheroni constant γ ≈ 0.5772. Z−1 is the inverse of the standard Normal cumulative distribution and e is Euler's number. The value grows with N without limit, but slowly, and stays below √(2 ln N).

With zero true Sharpe ratio, the annualised estimate over y years has standard deviation y−1/2 (eq. 3 with SR = 0). The expected maximum annual Sharpe ratio among N skill-less trials is therefore E[maxN] / √y (eq. 5). For N = 10 and one year this is –, the figure the paper reports as 1.57. Solving for y gives the minimum backtest length, MinBTL, the number of years required for the expected maximum to stay at a chosen level, here 1 (eq. 6):

MinBTL ≈ ( E[maxN]E[max SR̂] )2 < 2 ln NE[max SR̂]2

Read aloud: the minimum backtest length in years is the square of the expected maximum of N standard Normal draws divided by the Sharpe ratio that the best skill-less trial is allowed to reach. More trials require more years.

The paper's statement, computed here: with five years of data, at most – independent configurations can be tried before the best of them is expected to show an annual Sharpe ratio of 1 with no skill; with two years, –. The paper concludes that "a backtest which does not report the number of trials N used to identify the selected configuration makes it impossible to assess the risk of overfitting" (Bailey et al., 2014, p. 461).

5 · The deflated Sharpe ratio and the haircut

Bailey and López de Prado (2014) combine the two sources of inflation, selection among trials and non-Normal returns, in one statistic. The deflated Sharpe ratio is the probability that the true Sharpe ratio exceeds the threshold SR̂0 expected from the best of N skill-less trials (eq. 2). All Sharpe ratios in the formula are per period, not annualised.

DSR = Z [ (SR̂ − SR̂0) √T − 1√1 − γ̂3 SR̂ + γ̂4 − 14 SR̂2 ]
SR̂0 = √V[{SR̂n}] · ((1 − γ) Z−1[1 − 1/N] + γ Z−1[1 − e−1/N])

Read aloud: the deflated Sharpe ratio is the standard Normal probability of the gap between the estimate and the threshold, multiplied by the square root of the sample length minus one, and divided by a term that grows when returns are negatively skewed or fat-tailed. The threshold is the expected maximum of N skill-less trials, scaled by the standard deviation of the Sharpe ratios across the trials. A DSR above 0.95 is evidence at the 95% level that the selected strategy has a positive true Sharpe ratio.

Harvey and Liu (2015, eq. 3–5) take a p-value route. The single-test p-value of the t-statistic SR̂·√T is converted to a multiple-testing p-value for N independent tests, pM = 1 − (1 − pS)N, and the haircut Sharpe ratio is the Sharpe ratio whose single-test p-value equals pM. Their example: twenty years of monthly returns, an annual Sharpe ratio of 0.75, and 200 tests reduce the Sharpe ratio to about 0.32. Select a term for its definition.

6 · Non-Normal returns and drawdown

The Sharpe ratio uses two moments of the return distribution, the mean and the standard deviation. Harvey and Liu (2015, p. 13) state the limitation: "high observed Sharpe ratios could be the results of non-normal returns, for instance, an option-like strategy with high ex ante negative skew. In this case, Sharpe ratios should be viewed in the context of the skew." The DSR denominator includes the skewness γ̂3 and kurtosis γ̂4 for this reason: with negative skewness or kurtosis above 3, the same Sharpe ratio carries less evidence.

The Sharpe ratio is also unchanged by the order of the returns. A history in which the losses arrive consecutively and a history in which they are scattered have the same mean and standard deviation, and therefore the same Sharpe ratio, but different maximum drawdowns. The project reports the maximum drawdown from stats() next to every Sharpe ratio for this reason.

Terminology

Each entry: the technical definition first, then the same idea in everyday words in the orange strip.

Sharpe ratiomean excess return divided by the standard deviation of returns, usually annualised.how much a strategy earns for each unit of risk it takes. Two strategies with the same profit: the one with the smaller swings has the higher Sharpe ratio.
excess returnthe return minus the risk-free return over the same period.what the strategy earned beyond what cash would have earned with no risk.
risk-free ratethe return on a riskless asset, conventionally a short-term government bill.the interest that money in the safest place would earn.
annualisationconversion of a per-period statistic to annual units: mean × q, standard deviation × √q.turning a daily number into a yearly one so that different strategies can be compared on the same scale.
IIDindependent and identically distributed: each return is drawn from the same distribution, unaffected by earlier returns.each day's return is a fresh draw from the same distribution, with no memory of yesterday.
autocorrelation ρ(k)correlation between a return and the return k periods earlier.how much today's move tends to follow (positive) or reverse (negative) the move k days ago.
variance ratiovariance of q-period returns divided by q times the variance of one-period returns; 1 for uncorrelated returns.a check on whether risk adds up over time the simple way. Above 1: moves build on each other. Below 1: moves partly cancel.
standard errorstandard deviation of an estimator across repeated samples.how much the number would change if the same strategy were tested on a different stretch of history.
t-statistican estimate divided by its standard error; for the mean return it equals SR̂·√T.how many standard errors the result is away from zero. Larger means less likely to be chance.
p-valueprobability, under the null hypothesis, of a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed.the chance of getting a result this good if the strategy had no edge at all.
trialone backtested configuration of a rule and its parameters.one version of the idea that was tested. Changing a lookback from 63 to 126 days is a second trial.
selection biasinflation of a reported statistic caused by reporting only the best of several trials.showing only the best attempt makes the result look better than the method really is.
MinBTLminimum backtest length: years of data needed so that the best of N skill-less trials is not expected to exceed a given Sharpe ratio.how long the test history must be before trying N versions stops producing an impressive result by chance.
skewness γ̂₃third standardised moment of returns; negative when large losses are more likely than large gains of the same size.whether the bad surprises are bigger than the good ones. Negative skew: many small gains, occasional large losses.
kurtosis γ̂₄fourth standardised moment; 3 for the Normal distribution, higher when extreme returns are more frequent.how often very large moves happen compared with a bell curve.
DSRdeflated Sharpe ratio: the probability that the true Sharpe ratio exceeds the expected maximum of N skill-less trials, adjusted for skewness and kurtosis.the chance that the strategy is genuinely better than the best of the failures that were tested alongside it.
haircutthe percentage reduction from the reported Sharpe ratio to the multiple-testing-adjusted Sharpe ratio.how much of the reported result is removed once the number of tests is taken into account.
